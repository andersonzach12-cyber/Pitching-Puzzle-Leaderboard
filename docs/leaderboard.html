How uScore works

What it measures. uScore rates every pitch a pitcher throws by how unusual its physical characteristics are compared to everyone else who throws that same pitch type this season — a slider is only measured against other sliders, a sinker only against other sinkers, and so on, since "unique" means something different for each pitch shape.

What goes into it. For each pitch, we take velocity, induced vertical break (rise/drop), horizontal break (side-to-side movement), spin rate, and — where Statcast publishes it — active spin%, and measure how many standard deviations each one sits from the league average for that pitch type. Movement is weighted most heavily, since a pitch's shape and deception generally matter more than raw velocity for making it hard to hit. Horizontal break is normalized to a consistent "arm-side positive, glove-side negative" convention regardless of which hand a pitcher throws with, so left- and right-handed pitchers are directly comparable rather than being scored against a mixed-up, mirrored scale.

Why usage matters. The quotient shown here also factors in how often a pitcher actually throws that pitch. A pitch with freakish characteristics that's rarely used scores lower than an equally unique pitch that's a real, trusted weapon in the pitcher's mix — the goal is to reflect the pitch's actual impact, not just its raw numbers on paper.

Delivery matters too. Release characteristics — extension, arm angle, release height, horizontal release point — affect how hard every pitch a pitcher throws is to pick up, not just what the ball does after it leaves the hand. Every pitch's quotient is nudged up or down by a delivery modifier based on how unusual that pitcher's release point is relative to the rest of the league this season (1.00× is a perfectly average delivery). It's intentionally a gentle multiplier, not a dominant factor — a pitch's own velocity, movement, and spin still drive the vast majority of its score.

Data and sample size. Numbers come straight from Baseball Savant's tracked pitch data for the current regular season and postseason (spring training is excluded). A pitcher needs at least 25 tracked pitches of a given type this season to appear on that pitch type's leaderboard, to keep small, noisy samples out.

A caveat worth knowing. Pitch-type labels come straight from Statcast's own automatic classifier. For pitchers who throw an unusual, hybrid-shaped pitch, that classifier can occasionally split what's really one pitch across two labels (for example, tagging some of a pitcher's splitter-changeup hybrid as "Splitter" and some as "Changeup"). If a pitcher you'd expect to see isn't showing up where you'd guess, it's worth checking the neighboring pitch type.
